# Chapter 10 — Where Next (and How to Not Burn Out)

You now command: opening a terminal on any computer, walking around it,
pasting safely, installing software (Ollama, pip), reading errors, using
admin rights deliberately, and writing-and-running a real script. That is
*the whole foundation*. Everything else in your AI journey is decorating
this frame. Here's a gentle map of decoration.

## The golden path onward

In rough order of how they'll pay off for a beginner in 2026:

1. **Talk to your local model more.** Different models exist for different
   moods. Try `ollama run llama3.2:3b` when you want slightly smarter
   answers and have the RAM, or explore the "models" page on ollama.com
   (each listing page shows you the exact `ollama run ...` command to
   paste — the Chapter 3 gray-box rule covers you).
2. **Script your life a little.** One tiny Python script that renames a
   folder of files, or sums a CSV of book-sale numbers, or turns a journal
   into a nice markdown page. Your first script already contains 80% of
   the ideas needed for these.
3. **Learn just enough git to save your work** — the "track my chapters"
   skill, not the "software engineer" skill. It's maybe ten commands, and
   it means you can never *lose* work by experimenting bravely.
4. **Local AI utilities**: prompt library apps, offline "chat with my
   notes" tools, Ollama-adjacent helper scripts. These are squarely the
   kind of small, local, free things this manual's publisher builds and
   shares — and now you can *read* them and modify them.
5. **Only then** cloud AI and accounts — because by then you know what a
   terminal session is, so API keys and .env files will read as "a file
   where you keep one secret" instead of wizardry.

## If you only keep three habits from this manual

1. **Read before Enter.** The Chapter 3 habit. It's terminal safety, and
   it doubles as general internet safety — most online harm in life
   happens by moving too fast to read.
2. **`cd` home, `pwd` if unsure.** The rhythm that means you can never get
   spatially lost. This habit will save you dozens of micro-panics a
   year.
3. **Read the last line of every error.** Errors are the computer
   *answering*. Answer back. This one habit is why some people seem to
   "never get stuck."

## On feeling slow (to the nurse, the parent, the new learner)

You're learning a *new input language*, at the same time your life already
contains a thousand languages (someone else's medication schedule, someone
else's fear at 3 a.m., someone else's balance sheet). Being slow here isn't
a lack of intelligence; it's that your attention is heavily spent
elsewhere, by profession and by love. Terminal skills are unusual in that
they **compound quietly**: five honest minutes a week beats a heroic
Saturday that exhausts you. Do chapter-by-chapter. Skip days. Type the
walk-homework twice if that's what sticks. Speed is not the point;
*unreasoning fear is the enemy*, and it yields to exactly the small,
repeated touches you're doing.

## The three questions that will carry you

Wherever you go from here — tutorials, tools, AI agents, this publisher's
other guides — ask them of anything new:

1. **Where does my data go?** (Local-only is a perfectly valid answer, and
   often the *best* answer.)
2. **What does this command do in one sentence?**
3. **If I do this wrong, what's the cost, and is it reversible?**

That's terminal literacy in miniature: curiosity with a seatbelt.

## One last thing

Go back to your `terminal-practice` folder and run your
little script one more time. It'll list your own file and show the current
time — proof of one more day you showed up. Databases, agents, MCP servers,
local AI chat with your own notebooks — every single one of them is the
same shape as the thing you built today: a folder, a file, a command, a
result, understood.

You're not a beginner anymore. You're someone with a practice folder.
